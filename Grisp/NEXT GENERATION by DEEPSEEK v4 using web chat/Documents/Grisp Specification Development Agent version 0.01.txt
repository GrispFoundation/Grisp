# GRISP Specification Development Agent

## Specification v1.0

**Identifier:** GSDA/1.0
**Status:** Draft Technical Specification
**Purpose:** Cooperative development, analysis, repair, refinement, scoring, and publication of technical specifications using multiple LLM peers.

---

# 1. Purpose

GSDA/1.0 defines an AI system whose sole purpose is to help humans develop better technical specifications.

The system accepts:

* rough ideas;
* notes;
* requirements;
* existing specifications;
* partial specifications;
* competing proposals;
* design discussions;
* change requests.

It produces progressively improved specification artifacts through cooperative work by multiple LLM peers.

The system does **not** execute software, compile code, run programs, inspect runtime state, access external development tools, or determine whether an implementation works.

Its domain is exclusively:

> **Specification reasoning and specification improvement.**

The central mechanism is cooperative multi-LLM refinement.

Multiple LLMs independently propose interpretations and formulations, inspect one another's work, identify weaknesses, repair one another's specifications, and score competing results.

---

# 2. Central Principle

GSDA/1.0 is based on the following principle:

> **Many imperfect reasoning systems can cooperate to produce a better specification than one unreviewed reasoning system.**

The system therefore deliberately avoids the model:

```text
LLM A produces specification
        ↓
LLM A declares specification correct
        ↓
publish
```

Instead:

```text
Input
  ↓
Independent candidates
  ↓
Mutual critique
  ↓
Cross-repair
  ↓
Multi-dimensional scoring
  ↓
Reliability-weighted aggregation
  ↓
Further refinement
  ↓
Specification analysis
  ↓
Human review
  ↓
Publication
```

# 5. LLM Peer Model

An LLM peer is an independent reasoning participant.

A peer may perform one or more logical roles.

A peer is not considered authoritative merely because it:

* is more capable;
* has a higher score;
* has higher reliability;
* generated the original proposal;
* is the coordinator's preferred model.

Peers provide evidence for consideration.

---

# 6. Peer Roles

The following logical roles are defined.

## 6.1 Generator

Produces an independent specification candidate.

The Generator should attempt to solve the requested specification problem without relying on another peer's proposal.

---

## 6.2 Formalizer

Transforms informal ideas into explicit normative requirements.

Examples:

```text
MUST
MUST NOT
SHALL
SHALL NOT
SHOULD
SHOULD NOT
MAY
```

The Formalizer should identify hidden assumptions and make them explicit.

---

## 6.3 Critic

Attempts to find weaknesses.

A Critic should search for:

* contradictions;
* omissions;
* ambiguity;
* vague language;
* undefined terminology;
* conflicting requirements;
* incomplete edge cases;
* hidden assumptions;
* unnecessary constraints;
* accidental requirements.

---

## 6.4 Contradiction Analyst

Specializes in finding conflicts between requirements.

The Contradiction Analyst should distinguish:

```text
direct contradiction
potential contradiction
contextual tension
different scope
apparently similar but compatible requirement
```

---

## 6.5 Completeness Analyst

Attempts to identify things that the specification has failed to say.

The Completeness Analyst asks:

> What would an implementer, reviewer, user, or adversary still need to know?

---

## 6.6 Terminology Analyst

Maintains consistent definitions of domain terms.

The Terminology Analyst detects:

* undefined terms;
* duplicate terms;
* inconsistent definitions;
* terminology drift;
* overloaded terms.

---

## 6.7 Testability Analyst

Does not execute tests.

Instead, it examines whether each requirement is sufficiently precise to permit an objective future test.

It may propose:

* positive test concepts;
* negative test concepts;
* boundary cases;
* adversarial cases.

---

## 6.8 Cross-Repairer

Repairs another peer's specification.

This is a fundamental role.

A Cross-Repairer MUST be permitted to change another peer's work when it believes doing so improves the result.

---

## 6.9 Scorer

Scores proposed specifications and repairs using explicit criteria.

---

## 6.10 Adversarial Specification Reviewer

Attempts to construct interpretations under which the specification fails, becomes ambiguous, contradictory, incomplete, or exploitable.

---

## 6.11 Consolidator

Produces a new integrated specification from multiple compatible contributions.

The Consolidator MUST preserve provenance.

---

# 7. Peer Independence

Initial candidate generation SHOULD be independent.

Peers should not see each other's candidate answers during the initial generation phase unless the task explicitly requests collaborative generation from the beginning.

This preserves diversity.

After candidate generation, collaboration becomes desirable.

---

# 8. Cooperative Workflow

The default workflow is:

```text
1. Understand task
2. Generate candidates
3. Critique candidates
4. Cross-repair candidates
5. Score repairs
6. Aggregate results
7. Analyze resulting specification
8. Identify unresolved issues
9. Refine again
10. Repeat until convergence or termination
11. Produce final specification package
12. Human review
13. Publish
```

Not every task requires every role.

The coordinator dynamically chooses the appropriate workflow.

---

# 9. Candidate Generation

The coordinator sends the task to multiple peers.

Each peer produces:

```text
Candidate
{
    candidate_id
    peer_id
    task_id
    specification
    assumptions
    confidence
}
```

Candidates SHOULD be immutable.

---

# 10. Candidate Independence

The coordinator SHOULD encourage genuinely different approaches.

For example, with three peers:

```text
Peer A:
    minimal specification

Peer B:
    formal protocol-oriented specification

Peer C:
    adversarial/security-oriented specification
```

This diversity is valuable even if the final result is later merged.

---

# 11. Critique Phase

Each peer reviews other candidates.

A critique SHOULD contain:

```text
Critique
{
    critique_id
    critic_peer
    target_candidate
    findings[]
    severity
    confidence
    suggested_repairs[]
}
```

A finding SHOULD identify the exact problem whenever possible.

# 13. Cross-Repair

After critique, peers may repair candidates.

For N candidates and N repairers, the coordinator MAY produce N×N repairs.

Example:

```text
A → A
A → B
A → C

B → A
B → B
B → C

C → A
C → B
C → C
```

The source explicitly developed this matrix-based cross-repair model.

Cross-repair is preferred to self-repair-only because it permits one peer to recognize and correct an error that the original author failed to notice.

---

# 14. Repair Artifact

A repair MUST be represented as a new artifact.

```text
Repair
{
    repair_id
    repairer_peer
    target_artifact
    parent_artifacts[]
    specification
    changes[]
    rationale
}
```

The parent artifact MUST remain unchanged.

---

# 15. Repair Description

A repair SHOULD identify:

```text
added requirements
removed requirements
modified requirements
reworded requirements
new definitions
removed definitions
resolved contradictions
new open questions
```

This makes repair impact visible.

---

# 16. Mixed Repairs

A repair MAY combine ideas from multiple candidates.

Example:

```text
Candidate A
Candidate B
Candidate C
      \ | /
       \|/
        ↓
    Consolidated D
```

Such an artifact MUST preserve all known parent lineage.

No single peer may automatically be credited with all of D.

# 19. Requirement Extraction

The system SHOULD automatically identify normative statements from specification text.

Normative indicators include:

```text
MUST
MUST NOT
SHALL
SHALL NOT
SHOULD
SHOULD NOT
MAY
```

Extraction is deterministic where possible.

Semantic interpretation remains an LLM task.

# 20. Requirement Normalization

Peers SHOULD be asked to transform equivalent statements into normalized requirements.

For example:

```text
"The system should never accept malformed input."

becomes:

REQ-INPUT-001
The system MUST reject malformed input.
```

The original language SHOULD remain available as provenance.

# 21. Requirement Quality

Peers should evaluate every requirement for:


# 22. Requirement Coverage

The system SHOULD maintain:

```text

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

```

This helps detect ideas present in the original input that disappeared during consolidation.

# 23. Intent Preservation

An especially important function is preservation of the user's original intent.

The system SHOULD distinguish:

```text
USER INTENT
NORMATIVE REQUIREMENT
DESIGN DECISION
ASSUMPTION
OPTION
OPEN QUESTION
```

LLMs MUST NOT silently convert an assumption into a requirement.

Likewise, an important user requirement MUST NOT disappear merely because a peer considers it inconvenient.

# 27. Contradiction Analysis

Contradiction analysis operates at multiple levels.

### Direct

```text
A MUST do X.
B MUST NOT do X.
```

### Conditional

```text
A MUST do X when C.
B MUST NOT do X when C.
```

### Scope

```text
A applies to component X.
B applies to component Y.
```

The system MUST distinguish actual contradictions from merely similar statements.

---

# 28. Ambiguity Analysis

Peers SHOULD actively search for language that permits multiple interpretations.

Examples:

```text
"normally"
"appropriate"
"reasonable"
"as needed"
"soon"
"secure"
"valid"
"correct"
"small"
```

Such terms SHOULD be defined or removed when their ambiguity affects implementation.

---

# 29. Completeness Analysis

The system SHOULD ask:

```text
What happens when input is missing?
What happens when input is malformed?
What happens on failure?
What happens at boundaries?
What happens concurrently?
What happens when assumptions are false?
What happens when two requirements conflict?
What happens when an operation cannot complete?
```

This analysis is especially useful for finding omissions that conventional prose review misses.

---

# 30. Testability Analysis

The system does not execute tests.

Instead it determines whether requirements can eventually be checked.

For each requirement:

```text
TESTABLE
```

or:

```text
NOT CURRENTLY TESTABLE
```

If not testable, the reason MUST be recorded.

# 31. Test Concept


# 32. Revision Management

Every specification revision MUST have a revision identity.

A revision SHOULD reference its parent.

The system SHOULD produce:

```text

changed requirements
added definitions
removed definitions

new questions
```

# 33. Scoring Philosophy

GSDA uses **scoring rather than voting**.

Voting produces:

```text
A wins
```

Scoring preserves:

```text
how good
how confident
in what dimension
according to whom
```

The supplied design explicitly moved from voting toward scoring for this reason.

---

# 34. Multi-Dimensional Specification Score

Each peer SHOULD score every relevant candidate or repair.

The score SHOULD contain at least:

```text
correctness
completeness
consistency
clarity
precision
requirements_coverage
testability
coherence
simplicity
maintainability
```

Optional task-specific criteria may be added.

---

# 35. Criterion Scores

Each criterion uses:

```text
0.0 .. 1.0
```

Example:

```text
Correctness:          0.91

Consistency:          0.95
Clarity:              0.93
Testability:          0.81

```

# 36. Confidence

A peer MUST be able to distinguish:

```text
quality estimate
confidence in quality estimate
```

Example:

```text
quality = 0.92
confidence = 0.61
```

This means the peer considers the artifact strong but is uncertain about that judgment.

# 38. Reliability

Each peer SHOULD have an evolving reliability profile.

Reliability represents observed usefulness, not absolute truth.

It SHOULD be tracked separately for different activities.

Example:

```text
Peer A
  generation: 0.91
  critique:   0.84
  repair:     0.94
  scoring:    0.78
```

This prevents a peer's excellence in one activity from being incorrectly generalized to all activities.

---

# 39. Reliability Initialization

New peers SHOULD start with uncertainty rather than maximum trust.

The system SHOULD distinguish:

```text
known quality
unknown quality
confidence in quality
```

A peer with no history is not equivalent to a peer with a long record of strong performance.

---

# 40. Reliability Updating

Reliability MAY be updated from:

```text
quality of accepted contributions
quality of critiques
quality of repairs
agreement with later independent analysis
quality of requirement discoveries
quality of contradiction discoveries
quality of terminology corrections
quality of scoring predictions
```

Because this is a pure specification-development system, **there is no compiler, runtime, or external execution feedback**.

The outcome being measured is the subsequent quality of the specification work itself.

---

# 41. Reliability and Final Quality

A peer's reliability influences the weight assigned to its judgment.

Conceptually:

$$
W_i = R_i \times C_i
$$

where:

* \(R_i\) is peer reliability;
* \(C_i\) is confidence.

A peer with high confidence but historically poor judgment does not receive unlimited influence.

---

# 42. Detailed Aggregate Score

For a repair \(r\), let:

* \(S_{i,r}\) = peer \(i\)'s overall quality score;
* \(C_{i,r}\) = peer \(i\)'s confidence;
* \(R_i\) = peer \(i\)'s relevant reliability;
* \(W_i\) = optional criterion/role weight.

The aggregate score may be:

$$
A_r =
\frac{
\sum_i
R_i W_i C_{i,r} S_{i,r}
}{
\sum_i
R_i W_i C_{i,r}
}
$$

This is preferable to a simple sum because the result remains on the same 0..1 scale.

---

# 43. Criterion-Level Aggregation

The coordinator SHOULD also aggregate each criterion separately.

For criterion \(k\):

$$
A_{r,k} =
\frac{
\sum_i
R_{i,k} C_{i,r,k} S_{i,r,k}
}{
\sum_i
R_{i,k} C_{i,r,k}
}
$$

This allows the coordinator to recognize:

```text
excellent consistency
poor completeness
excellent clarity
weak testability
```

rather than hiding everything in one number.

---

# 44. Score Variance

The coordinator SHOULD calculate disagreement.

For example:

```text
Completeness:
A = 0.94
B = 0.91
C = 0.42
D = 0.89
```

The large disagreement is itself useful.

The coordinator SHOULD flag high-variance criteria.

---

# 45. Minority Findings

A minority critique MUST NOT be discarded merely because most peers disagree.

A single peer may discover an important problem.

Therefore:

```text
minority finding
+
high severity
+
credible rationale
```

SHOULD trigger additional investigation.

---

# 46. Score Manipulation Resistance

A peer MUST NOT gain influence simply by assigning extreme scores.

The system SHOULD monitor:

```text
score inflation
score compression
excessive confidence
systematic disagreement
systematic agreement
```

A peer that always assigns:

```text
0.99
```

provides less useful information than a well-calibrated peer.

---

# 47. Score Calibration

Over time the coordinator SHOULD compare prior scores with subsequent independent judgments.

For example:

```text
Peer A predicted:
repair X = 0.90

Later independent review:
repair X = strong

Peer A prediction was well calibrated.
```

This may improve score-reliability.

---

# 48. Reliability Must Be Multi-Dimensional

At minimum:

```text
generation_reliability
critique_reliability
repair_reliability
scoring_reliability
```

The system SHOULD additionally support:

```text
completeness_reliability
contradiction_reliability
terminology_reliability
testability_reliability
```

---

# 49. Lineage-Aware Reliability

When a final specification contains contributions from several peers, reliability updates SHOULD account for lineage.

Example:

```text
A creates requirement X
B improves X
C restructures X
D integrates X into final specification
```

All four may deserve some attribution.

The system SHOULD avoid simplistic:

```text final artifact = D's work
```

when the provenance graph demonstrates otherwise.

# 51. Reliability Update for Mixed Contributions

For a mixed artifact:

$$
Credit_p =
Contribution_p
\times
OutcomeQuality
$$

The exact deterministic update function SHOULD be defined by the implementation.

Unknown contribution MUST remain unknown rather than being invented.

---

# 52. Specification Quality Loop

The system should operate as a closed reasoning loop:

```text
generate
   ↓
criticize
   ↓
repair
   ↓
score
   ↓
identify weaknesses
   ↓
refine
   ↓
score again
```

Unlike a one-shot generation system, the output is expected to improve over multiple rounds.

---

# 53. Progressive Refinement

Later rounds SHOULD focus on unresolved weaknesses rather than regenerating the entire specification blindly.

Example:

```text
Round 1:
    general architecture

Round 2:
    requirements precision

Round 3:
    contradiction resolution

Round 4:
    edge cases

Round 5:
    terminology

Round 6:
    traceability

Round 7:
    final adversarial review
```

The coordinator MAY dynamically determine the next focus.

---

# 54. Repair Targeting

Repairs SHOULD be targeted when possible.

Instead of:

> Rewrite everything.

the peer may receive:

```text
Target:
    REQ-SECURITY-014

Findings:
    ambiguous authorization boundary
    conflicting definition in section 7
```

and produce a targeted repair.

---

# 55. Consolidation

After multiple repairs, the coordinator MAY request consolidation.

The consolidator receives:

```text
candidate specification
multiple repairs
critiques
scores
terminology
requirements
open questions
```

and produces a new specification artifact.

---

# 56. Consolidation Constraint

Consolidation MUST NOT silently erase disagreement.

Where peers disagree:

```text
Alternative A
Alternative B
```

the specification SHOULD either:

* choose one with rationale;
* define conditions under which each applies;
* or create an open question.

---

# 57. No Forced Consensus

The system MUST NOT require all peers to agree.

The objective is:

> **best-supported specification**, not unanimous agreement.

---

# 58. Convergence

The coordinator may consider the process converged when:

```text
major defects have been addressed
requirement coverage is stable
terminology is stable
scores stabilize
major disagreements are resolved
no new high-severity findings appear
```

Convergence does not mean perfect certainty.

---

# 59. Stagnation

The coordinator SHOULD detect when repeated rounds stop producing meaningful improvement.

Indicators include:

```text
same findings
same repairs
same scores
same disagreements
no new requirements
```

On stagnation, the coordinator MAY:

* introduce a new peer;
* change peer specialization;
* request a fresh independent candidate;
* focus on adversarial analysis;
* ask the human for clarification.

---

# 60. Human Clarification

When a problem cannot be resolved from the available information, the system SHOULD ask the human rather than invent an answer.

Examples:

```text
REQ-A conflicts with business goal.

Priority unknown.

Ask human.
```

The resulting answer becomes part of the specification provenance.

---

# 61. Human Authority

The human remains authoritative over:

* intended goals;
* ambiguous business decisions;
* unresolved trade-offs;
* scope;
* priorities;
* publication.

LLMs assist with reasoning.

They do not replace the human's intent.

This greatly reduces accidental conversion of speculation into specification.

# 75. No Hidden Decisions

The coordinator MUST NOT silently choose between materially different alternatives.

Every substantive consolidation decision SHOULD have:

```text
decision
basis

```

# 77. Specification Quality Score

A high-level score MAY be calculated:

$$
Q = \sum_k w_k A_k
$$

where:

* \(A_k\) is the aggregate criterion score;
* \(w_k\) is the criterion weight.

The weights MUST be task-specific or explicitly defined.

The score is a ranking aid, not an objective proof of specification correctness.

# 78. Hard Findings

Certain findings SHOULD be treated as blocking:

```text
direct contradiction
missing critical requirement
undefined critical term
unresolved critical ambiguity
loss of explicit user requirement
scope contradiction
```

A specification with unresolved blocking findings MUST NOT be marked publication-ready.

---

# 79. Soft Findings

Other findings may be advisory:

```text
style
verbosity
minor wording
optional simplification
alternative organization
```

Soft findings need not block convergence.

# 84. Prompt Diversity

The coordinator SHOULD vary peer instructions enough to avoid all peers producing the same analysis.

Examples:


# 85. Adversarial Specification Mode

An adversarial peer SHOULD attempt to exploit the specification without implementing anything.

It asks:

```text
Can this sentence be interpreted two ways?
Can two requirements conflict?
Can an implementer satisfy the letter but violate the intent?
Is a required concept undefined?
Is an important case omitted?
```

# 86. Implementer Simulation

Although GSDA never implements the specification, peers MAY simulate an implementer's perspective.

A peer may ask:

> “Could two competent engineers implement this specification differently?”

If yes, it should identify why.

This is specification analysis, not implementation.

# 87. Reviewer Simulation


This creates useful perspective diversity.

# 88. No External Reality Requirement

GSDA/1.0 deliberately does not require an external oracle.

It cannot determine whether a specification is objectively correct in every domain.

Its purpose is instead to maximize:

```text

consistency
traceability
explicitness
reasoning diversity
```

while exposing unresolved uncertainty.

# 90. Evidence Classes

# 91. Scoring Does Not Establish Truth

A specification can receive:

```text
0.97
```

and still be wrong.

A score means:

> The peer network currently considers this candidate stronger according to the defined criteria.

It does not mean:

> The specification has been objectively proven correct.

---

# 92. Reliability Does Not Establish Truth

A highly reliable peer can still be wrong.

Reliability only changes the influence of the peer's judgment based on historical performance.

---

# 93. No Majority Rule

A result MUST NOT be accepted simply because:

```text
8 peers agree
2 peers disagree
```

The two dissenting peers may have found the most important problem.

---

# 94. No Highest-Score Blindness

```text

confidence

lineage
```

---

# 95. Candidate Preservation

The coordinator SHOULD preserve competing candidates long enough to support meaningful comparison.

Prematurely deleting alternatives destroys useful reasoning diversity.

# 106. Example End-to-End Session

Input:

### Round 8

### Round 9

A new immutable specification revision is created.

# 110. Example Mixed Repair

# 111. Why Cross-Repair Matters

Self-repair encourages an agent to defend its own assumptions.

Cross-repair encourages independent reconsideration.

The system therefore SHOULD expose candidates to peers with different:

* reasoning styles;
* assumptions;
* specializations;
* models;
* providers.

This increases the probability that hidden defects are noticed.

---

# 112. Why Scoring Matters

Voting compresses:

```text
0.99
0.90
0.52
0.10
```

into:

```text
winner
```

Scoring retains information.

GSDA therefore uses scores to preserve:

* disagreement;
* relative quality;
* confidence;
* criterion-specific strengths;
* criterion-specific weaknesses.

---

# 113. Why Reliability Matters

Not every peer is equally useful for every task.

Historical observations can reveal:

```text
who finds omissions
who writes precise requirements
who detects contradictions
who produces good repairs
who scores well
```

The system can therefore become better at selecting useful perspectives.

---

# 114. Why Lineage Matters

Without lineage:

```text
final specification
```

has no explanation of how it emerged.

With lineage:

```text
idea
→ candidate
→ critique
→ repair
→ consolidation
→ final revision
```

the evolution of the specification becomes auditable.

---

# 115. Why Iteration Matters

A specification should not be judged solely after the first generation.

The cooperative system should repeatedly ask:

> What is still wrong?

until the expected value of another round becomes low.

---

# 116. Termination

The coordinator MUST terminate when:

```text
human accepts
resource limit reached
maximum rounds reached
convergence reached
stagnation detected
blocking ambiguity requires human input
```

A maximum round limit SHOULD exist to prevent endless refinement.

# 120. Final Principle

GSDA/1.0 is not intended to find a single AI that is good enough to write specifications alone.

It is intended to create a system in which:

```text
one AI proposes
another challenges
another notices omissions
another repairs
another improves the repair
another consolidates
all score
reliability determines influence
lineage preserves contribution
the coordinator detects disagreement
the system iterates
the human decides intent
```

The fundamental loop is therefore:

> **Generate → Critique → Cross-Repair → Score → Refine → Repeat.**

The purpose of the system is not to manufacture artificial certainty.

Its purpose is to systematically turn incomplete human ideas into increasingly precise, internally coherent, traceable, reviewable specifications while preserving uncertainty and disagreement wherever they genuinely remain.
